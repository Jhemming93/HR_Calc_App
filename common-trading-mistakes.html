Common trading mistakes

Most bad trades do not come from complicated math. They usually come from using weak assumptions, forgetting costs, or letting urgency override the numbers.

1. Treating the highest listing as the market price

A seller can ask any amount. That does not mean a buyer will pay it. Use several observations and test a conservative resale price before buying.

2. Forgetting seller fees

The visible difference between buy and sell prices is not your profit. Calculate the amount you keep after the selected selling fee, then subtract your acquisition cost.

3. Ignoring break-even

If you do not know your break-even sale price, it is easy to negotiate below it by accident. Check the break-even output before deciding how low you are willing to go.

4. Assuming rarity guarantees value

Rarity can affect supply, but demand determines whether someone wants to pay your target price. A rare item with weak demand can be difficult to sell.

5. Using Grab cost incorrectly

The cost of one spin is not necessarily the economic cost of obtaining a particular rare item. When analyzing a Grab, use expected value across all outcomes. When analyzing an item you already own, use a consistent cost basis.

6. Leaving bad outcomes out of expected value

A Grab calculation must account for every possible outcome. Omitting low-value items makes the average look artificially strong.

7. Buying without considering liquidity

An item can be “worth” a high amount on paper but still take a long time to find a buyer. Gold locked in slow inventory cannot be used for other opportunities.

8. Chasing a deal because it looks cheap

A discount only matters relative to a realistic resale value. An item marked far below an unrealistic listing can still be overpriced.

9. Failing to re-check the market

Prices can move between purchase and sale. Re-evaluate your estimate if new supply appears, the item is re-released, or the listing sits longer than expected.

10. Negotiating without a minimum price

Decide your floor before the conversation starts. That floor should be based on break-even and your desired profit, not on how persuasive the other person is.

11. Confusing gross profit with ROI

A large Gold profit can require a very large investment. Compare the profit with the amount of Gold committed so you can judge capital efficiency.

12. Letting sunk cost control the decision

If an item's market value falls after you buy it, the original purchase price does not force the market to recover. Reassess the current choice: hold, lower the price, or redeploy the Gold when possible.

Pre-trade checklist

  • Do I know my actual acquisition cost?
  • Is my resale estimate based on more than one listing?
  • Have I included the current selling fee?
  • Do I know my break-even price?
  • Is there enough demand for this item to sell in a reasonable time?
  • Does the trade still work under a conservative resale scenario?
  • Am I comfortable holding the item if the market moves against me?
The calculator is most useful when you challenge your own assumptions. Try the optimistic case, but always run the conservative case too.
