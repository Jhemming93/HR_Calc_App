Understanding ROI on item flips

Profit tells you how much Gold you made. Return on investment, or ROI, tells you how large that profit was compared with the amount of Gold you had to commit.

The basic idea

If you spend 1,000g and receive 1,200g after selling costs, your profit is 200g. That 200g is 20% of the 1,000g you invested. ROI is useful because it makes trades of different sizes easier to compare.

ROI formula: profit ÷ purchase cost × 100. Use profit after applicable selling fees, not the difference between gross listing prices.

Why Gold profit alone can be misleading

A 2,000g profit sounds better than a 500g profit, but the larger trade may require far more capital. A 500g profit on a 1,000g purchase is a much larger percentage return than a 2,000g profit on a 20,000g purchase.

ROI and turnover work together

ROI does not include time by itself. A strong percentage return that takes months to realize may be less useful than a smaller return that can be repeated frequently. This is sometimes called capital efficiency: how effectively your available Gold is being used over time.

Break-even comes first

Before thinking about ROI, determine the sale price needed to recover your purchase cost after fees. If the expected sale price is below break-even, the ROI is negative. The calculator's break-even output gives you the minimum sale target before profit begins.

Target profit versus target ROI

Highrise Gold Lab currently lets you enter a desired profit in Gold. You can also think in percentage terms. If you usually want a 25% return, convert that target into Gold before entering it. For example, on a 4,000g purchase, a 25% target corresponds to 1,000g of profit.

Three ways to compare two potential flips

  • Absolute profit: which one could add more Gold?
  • ROI: which one produces more profit for each Gold invested?
  • Turnover: which one is more likely to sell quickly enough to free your Gold for the next trade?

Do not ignore downside

A high projected ROI can come from a very low purchase price, but that does not mean the resale estimate is reliable. If the item is difficult to sell or its price is volatile, the apparent return may be compensating you for more uncertainty.

Use scenarios instead of one forecast

Run a conservative sale price, a normal sale price, and an optimistic sale price. Compare the resulting profit and ROI. If the trade remains acceptable in the conservative case, the decision is less dependent on one perfect outcome.

When a lower ROI can still make sense

A lower-percentage trade may still be useful if it is highly liquid, requires little effort, or uses Gold that would otherwise sit idle. ROI is a comparison tool, not the only factor in a decision.

A good habit is to record both the expected profit and the amount of Gold committed. That makes it easier to notice when large trades are producing surprisingly small percentage returns.
